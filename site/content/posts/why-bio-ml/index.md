AI has been improving at an accelerating pace, and I've had the luck to watch that happen up close in biology. Over my PhD, working on the RFdiffusion series of models, we went in only a few years from coaxing plausible backbones out of a model to designing proteins that bind, catalyse, and assemble roughly as we intended.

What that progress really gives us is the ability to *steer* how biomolecules interact. For most of its history, biology has been a science of discovery: we find what nature has already made, then work out how it works. Protein design is turning it into a science of rational engineering, where we decide what we want a molecule to do and then build it. That shift has made science a lot of fun. Scientists who dream big can take on audacious problems, from artificial photosynthesis to molecular motors, and the same capabilities are speeding up the development of new medicines and materials. The gap between "wouldn't it be amazing if…" and something you can test in the lab has never been smaller.

Many people I talk to outside AI research are anxious about where the technology is heading, both about safety and about what it means for society. I think part of the reason is that most of them haven't yet seen what is happening in biology. Working at the frontier of the life sciences therefore feels like a responsibility to the public good: to show, concretely, that the benefits of AI can reach everyone, and to give people a reason for hope rather than only for worry.

Doing this well means advancing capabilities carefully, and biology comes with its own particular dangers. Tools that design novel biomolecular interactions sit close to capabilities the field has good reason to be cautious about — mirror-image biology being the clearest example, where the same chemistry that makes mirror proteins promising as therapeutics is what makes self-replicating mirror organisms a serious biosecurity concern. The capability and the hazard grow from the same root, which is why the people building these tools have to think about access, evaluation, and misuse as seriously as they think about benchmarks.

This is why I'm encouraged when AI labs take both halves of this seriously: pushing capabilities forward for everyone's benefit while working out how to give the wider scientific community safe access to them. Anthropic has been a good example. I've been excited by its recent work in biology, and I've watched Claude change how protein design gets done day to day, from writing pipelines to reasoning through design campaigns.

I want to build these tools alongside people who share those goals: developing AI's biological capabilities safely, and using them to advance human health for the good of all. I can't think of a more hopeful place to work in AI right now.

-Jasper
